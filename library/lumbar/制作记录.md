### 图是怎么生成的

1. `figures/tools/project-outlines.py` 把 Z-Anatomy 模型（与肩部 3D 同一份 `z-anatomy-1.4.0` 模型）里 T6–L5 椎骨、第 6 至第 12 肋、骶骨、尾骨、髋骨、右下肢骨，以及腰方肌和三层腹壁肌的网格投影成前、后、外、上四个方向的二维轮廓，以及正中矢状切面和 L3、L4 横断面，写到 `figures/tools/outlines.json`。
2. `figures/tools/lumbarfig.py` 是画图工具：按视角摆放骨的轮廓、画起止点、贴中英标签并自动避让。它照髋部的 `hipfig.py` 改写，建在共用的 `daily/engine/figlib/fig.py` 之上，所以知识库和每日学习的图风格一致。
3. `figures/build-figures.py` 登记每张图（骨 `figs_bones.py`、肌肉 `figs_muscles.py`、横断面 `figs_section.py`、神经 `figs_nerves.py`、运动 `figs_motion.py`、临床与穴位 `figs_clinical.py`），并写出 `figure-labels.json`。
4. 重画：`python3 library/lumbar/figures/build-figures.py [图号…]`。
5. 肌肉的起止点画在骨的轮廓上，但肌腹的形状是示意（模型里只有腰方肌一块本章肌肉的网格）。

## 3. 需要 gui 知道的未核实项

- **骨盆倾斜的力偶**（图 13，3.4 节）：打开的资料都没有把它写成“力偶”。图 13 标明“教学模型”，腘绳肌标“未核实”；正文只写有来源的部分（下交叉综合征是理论构想，腹直肌参与骨盆后倾，深层腹肌可能协助臀大肌控制骨盆前旋）。
- **腹肌作为腰椎屈肌**：查证资料里没有，所以 3.1 屈曲一行只写重力、竖脊肌离心收缩和腰大肌（有争议）。
- **腰方肌在吸气时固定第 12 肋**：只作为经典说法，正文写明有争议（Kenhub 正文说它辅助呼气，StatPearls 认为活动的第 12 肋使它无法把力传给膈）。“髋上提（hip hiking）”没在打开的网页上找到，没写。
- **神经根出口规律**：写成“每条腰神经根在同序数椎骨下方穿出，绕过本节段椎间盘上方的椎弓根”（StatPearls、Radiopaedia）。L4–L5 旁中央型突出压 L5 根、极外侧型压 L4 根有资料直接写；L3–L4 突出压 L4 根是按同一规律推出来的。
- **背根神经节位置**：只写“在椎间孔内或其附近，下腰椎更常在孔内”；“在椎弓根下方”没有确认，删去。
- **腰椎骨盆节律**：经典说法是弯腰时腰骶部先屈、骨盆再前倾，起身时骨盆先动。打开的资料没有检验这个严格的先后顺序（Descarreaux 等 2010 只与起身时主要从髋部开始相符），也没有给出腰与髋的比例，所以图 14 写成“经典模型”。
- **腰骶关节的活动度**：Kenhub 写青春期前腰骶关节约占腰椎屈伸总范围的 75%（约 18°），与尸体研究和两个网站给出的总范围对不上，正文写明。
- **脊髓圆锥的平面**：各资料从约 T12–L1 到 L2 不等，正文写出差异。
- **嵴上线（两侧髂嵴最高点连线）的平面**：多写在第 4 腰椎棘突，也有资料写 L3–L4 间隙或 L4–L5 椎间盘；男女差异两种说法方向相反，正文都写了出处。
- **棘上韧带的下端**：有的写约在 L3–L4，有的写到骶骨；图 3 用虚线并写明“各资料不一”。
- **第 12 肋上腰方肌和膈的附着**、T12 作为过渡椎的具体特征、腰椎前凸角的正常值：都没在打开的网页上确认，没写。
- **竖脊肌的脊髓节段**：只有 Physiopedia 一处给出（腰髂肋肌 T7–L3，胸最长肌 T7–L5）；胸棘肌、回旋肌等没有找到节段，正文写明。
- **多裂肌触诊**：没有资料描述用手直接触诊多裂肌肌腹，4.5 节用的是一项超声研究的体位和标记方法，正文写明。

### 骨、关节与韧带（b1）

来源之间的出入（discrepancies）：

- Level of the iliac crest line: Dartmouth (Chapter 39) and the MSD Manual place it at the L4 spinous process (MSD: sometimes L3 in women); Kenhub (Iliac crest) says it passes through the L4 vertebra; Radiopaedia (Intercristal line) says Tuffier described it through the L4/L5 disc and that on radiographs it varies from the inferior endplate of L4 to the superior endplate of L5, usually lower in women; Physiopedia (Pelvic Landmarks) gives around L3–L4; the Queen's University lumbar puncture module says it crosses the L3–L4 interspace. Records say 'usually the L4 spinous process' and name the range.
- Lower end of the supraspinous ligament: Kenhub (Supraspinous ligament) says C7 to L3/L4, merging with the thoracolumbar fascia; Kenhub (Intervertebral joint) says from C7 until the third or fourth lumbar vertebra; Elsevier Complete Anatomy says it reaches only as far as L3–L4 and is replaced below L4 by latissimus dorsi fibres; Kenhub (Joints and ligaments of the vertebral column) and WSU (The vertebral column) say it runs from C7 to the sacrum, and Physiopedia (Sacrum) gives the median sacral crest as an attachment. No opened source used the brief's 'L4/L5' wording.
- Number of coccygeal vertebrae: TeachMeAnatomy (Coccyx) says four; Dartmouth says usually four; Kenhub (Vertebral column) says three to four; StatPearls (Sacral vertebrae) says 3 to 5.
- Number of sacral vertebrae: StatPearls, TeachMeAnatomy and Kenhub say five; Dartmouth says five (sometimes six).
- Annulus fibrosus lamellae: Kenhub (Intervertebral discs) and Physiopedia (Lumbar Anatomy) give 15–25; Radiopaedia (Annulus fibrosus) gives 15 to 20. Fibre angle: Kenhub gives about 60 degrees to the vertical axis and Physiopedia a 30 degree angle between the vertebral bodies; we read these as the same obliquity measured from different reference lines, but neither page says so.
- End of the spinal cord: StatPearls (Lumbar spine) and StatPearls (Sacral vertebrae) give the conus medullaris at the L1–L2 vertebrae; TeachMeAnatomy (Lumbar spine) says around L1 in one place and around L1–L2 in another; the MSD Manual says the cord ends at approximately T12–L1.
- Facet joint innervation: Radiopaedia (Facet joint) and Physiopedia (Lumbar Anatomy) name the medial branch of the dorsal ramus, each branch supplying its own level and the joint below; Kenhub (Intervertebral joint) says the disc and facet joints are innervated by the sinuvertebral nerve, with the facet joint additionally supplied by the medial branch of the posterior ramus.
- Accessory process direction: Radiopaedia (Lumbar spine) says it projects posteriorly from the root of the transverse process; Dartmouth says it may project inferiorly from the root; Kenhub, TeachMeAnatomy and Physiopedia place it on the posterior surface of the base of the transverse process without giving a direction.
- Lumbar facet orientation wording: StatPearls, Kenhub (Joints and ligaments) and Radiopaedia (Facet joint) say sagittal; Radiopaedia (Lumbar spine) says obliquely oriented and cylindrically shaped; Kenhub (Intervertebral joint) says the superior processes are vertical, concave and oriented posteromedially in the coronal plane; TeachMeAnatomy says nearly vertical. Permitted movement: Kenhub and Radiopaedia say rotation is limited; Physiopedia says lateral flexion is permitted and rotation prevented; Elsevier (Joints of Vertebral Column) lists flexion, extension, lateral flexion and rotation as lumbar movements. Kenhub (Lumbar vertebrae) says the superior facets face medially and the inferior facets laterally; Elsevier (Lumbar vertebrae) and Kenhub (Intervertebral joint) say posteromedially and anterolaterally; Physiopedia (Lumbar vertebrae) says 'posteromedially or medially' and 'anterolaterally or laterally'. Kenhub (Joints and ligaments) adds that limited flexion, extension and lateral flexion are still possible.
- Iliolumbar ligament origin and bands: Radiopaedia says L5 transverse process in most cases; Elsevier says L5 and sometimes L4; Physiopedia's description names L4 and L5 but its anatomy section lists only L5. Physiopedia names anterior and posterior bands, Elsevier names upper and lower bands.
- Intervertebral foramen: Radiopaedia places the vertebral body and disc at the anterior boundary and the facet joint capsule posteriorly; the Kenhub page lists the posterior aspects of the bodies and discs together with the facet joint capsule without clearly assigning anterior and posterior borders, and calls the foramen oval-shaped, while Radiopaedia describes a keyhole appearance on sagittal MRI.
- Vertebral endplate and the disc: Radiopaedia (Vertebral body endplate) says the endplate is now generally considered part of the vertebral body, although it was historically grouped with the intervertebral disc; Kenhub (Intervertebral discs) describes the nucleus as sandwiched by cartilage endplates within its account of the disc; Elsevier (Intervertebral disc L4–L5) and TeachMeAnatomy (Vertebral column) list only the annulus fibrosus and nucleus pulposus as disc components.
- Upper attachment of the anterior longitudinal ligament: Radiopaedia says it ascends to the anterior arch of C1, where it becomes the anterior atlanto-occipital membrane; Kenhub (Joints and ligaments) says it attaches to the occipital bone anterior to the foramen magnum; Physiopedia (Lumbar Anatomy) says it originates from the anterior and basilar aspect of the occiput.
- Thoracolumbar transition: Dartmouth (Chapter 39) says T11 and T12 are transitional, partly resembling lumbar vertebrae; Kenhub (Vertebral column) says the last four thoracic vertebrae (T9–T12) share some characteristics with the lumbar spine.
- Elastin content of the ligamentum flavum: Physiopedia (Ligamentum flavum) gives 80% elastin and 20% collagen; Physiopedia (Lumbar Anatomy) gives an elastin content of more than 80%.
- Disc nutrition: Kenhub (Intervertebral joint) and Radiopaedia (Vertebral body endplate) describe nutrients reaching the avascular disc by diffusion from the endplate; Kenhub (Intervertebral discs) says the nutrients are absorbed from circulating blood by osmosis.

### 竖脊肌、下后锯肌与胸腰筋膜（m1）

来源之间的出入（discrepancies）：

- Iliocostalis lumborum rib insertion: Kenhub gives the angles of ribs 5–12 (plus L1–L4 transverse processes); Complete Anatomy (Elsevier) gives the angles of ribs 5–10 (plus L1–L4 transverse processes); StatPearls 'Anatomy, Back, Muscles' and Physiopedia 'Erector Spinae' give the angles of ribs 4–12; Physiopedia 'Iliocostalis Lumborum' gives the inferior borders of the angles of the lower 6 or 7 ribs and does not mention the lumbar transverse processes. The record says 'usually ribs 5–12'.
- Iliocostalis lumborum origin: Kenhub lists the lateral sacral crest, the medial end of the iliac crest and the thoracolumbar fascia; Complete Anatomy lists the posterior one third of the iliac crest and the posterolateral sacrum; Physiopedia 'Iliocostalis Lumborum' lists a broad tendon on the medial sacral crest plus lumbar and T11–T12 spinous processes and the iliac crest; Physiopedia 'Erector Spinae' lists only the iliac crest.
- Erector spinae segments: only Physiopedia gives segments (iliocostalis lumborum T7–L3; longissimus thoracis T7–L5); Kenhub, Complete Anatomy, TeachMeAnatomy and Radiopaedia name only the regions (cervical, thoracic, lumbar). No opened source gave segments for spinalis thoracis.
- Longissimus thoracis origin: Physiopedia 'Longissimus Thoracis' gives the transverse and mamillary processes of the lumbar vertebrae and the lumbosacral aponeurosis; Kenhub gives the lumbar intermuscular aponeurosis (lumbar portion) and the L1–L5 spinous and transverse processes, sacrum and posterior iliac crest (thoracic portion); Complete Anatomy gives the spinous processes of all lumbar vertebrae, the dorsal sacrum and the posterior iliac crest.
- Spinalis thoracis origin: Kenhub and Physiopedia give the T11–L2 spinous processes, whereas Complete Anatomy gives T11–L3. Radiopaedia names the attachments in the reverse direction, listing upper thoracic spinous processes as the origin and the T11–L2 spinous processes as the insertion.
- Common tendon of the erector spinae: TeachMeAnatomy and StatPearls call it a common tendinous origin; Radiopaedia notes that several texts describe it as a single common insertion on the back of the sacrum; Willard et al. (2012) describe the erector spinae aponeurosis as the common tendon of the erector and multifidi muscles; Physiopedia says it blends with the thoracolumbar fascia.
- Unilateral erector spinae action: Kenhub, TeachMeAnatomy and Complete Anatomy describe only ipsilateral lateral flexion; Physiopedia 'Erector Spinae' adds ipsilateral rotation of the vertebral column and head, and Physiopedia 'Spinalis Thoracis' lists rotation.
- Serratus posterior inferior innervation: Kenhub's text gives the anterior rami of T9–T11 plus the subcostal nerve (T12), while its summary table says T9–T12; Complete Anatomy gives the anterior rami of T9–T12; Physiopedia gives the ventral rami of the intercostal nerves of T9–T12.
- Serratus posterior inferior insertion: Kenhub says the inferoposterior aspect of ribs 9–12 just lateral to their angles; Complete Anatomy says four tendons to the outer surfaces of ribs 9–12, lateral to the angles on ribs 9–11; Physiopedia says the lower border of ribs 9–12.
- Serratus posterior inferior function: Kenhub and Complete Anatomy describe rib depression assisting (forced) expiration; Physiopedia states that there is no electromyographic evidence for a respiratory role; Complete Anatomy adds a possible proprioceptive role.
- Serratus posterior inferior palpation: Hands-on Anatomy (Temple) gives a prone palpation route lateral to the lumbar and lower thoracic vertebrae toward the lower ribs, whereas Physiopedia states that the serratus posterior muscles cannot be palpated (the statement appears in the context of the superior muscle).
- StatPearls 'Anatomy, Back' (NBK539746) lists C7–T3 spinous processes, ribs 2–5 and T1–T4 under the label serratus posterior inferior and T11–L3, ribs 9–12 and T9–T12 under the label serratus posterior superior, which appears to swap the two muscles; Kenhub, Complete Anatomy and Physiopedia agree that the inferior muscle runs from T11–L2 to ribs 9–12.
- Thoracolumbar fascia layers: Willard et al. (2012), Schuenke et al. (2012), Physiopedia, Complete Anatomy and StatPearls describe three lumbar layers, while noting that older texts (for example Rosse and Gaddum-Rosse, cited by Schuenke et al.) describe only two.
- Erector spinae innervation branches: Kenhub (erector spinae, iliocostalis and spinalis pages) and Complete Anatomy name the lateral branches of the dorsal rami for the group; StatPearls 'Anatomy, Back, Muscles' and Radiopaedia say that at the lumbar level the lateral branches supply the iliocostalis and the intermediate branches supply the longissimus; Kenhub's longissimus page gives lateral and intermediate branches for the lumbar portion and medial and lateral branches for the thoracic portion; TeachMeAnatomy and Physiopedia 'Erector Spinae' say only the posterior (dorsal) rami.
- Mnemonic: the task suggested 'I Love Spine', but no opened source gives it. Kenhub gives 'I Like Standing' (lateral to medial); Radiopaedia lists 'I Like Standing', 'I Long for Spinach', 'I Like Siri' and one other variant (not 'I Love Spine'), noting they usually run lateral to medial.
- Longissimus thoracis insertion: Kenhub's longissimus page text gives T1–T6 and T7–T12 transverse processes plus adjacent rib angles, while its summary table gives T1–T12 transverse processes and the angles of ribs 7–12 (and its text gives the lumbar portion as L1–L4 plus a separate L5 fascicle, against L1–L5 in the table); Kenhub's erector spinae page says the thoracic vertebrae and the inferior six ribs; Complete Anatomy's quick facts say the second to twelfth ribs while its detailed text says the posterior ends of all twelve ribs; Physiopedia 'Longissimus Thoracis' says the transverse processes of all thoracic vertebrae and the lower 9 or 10 ribs; Physiopedia 'Erector Spinae' says T1–T12 transverse processes and the angles of ribs 7–12.
- Serratus posterior inferior origin: Kenhub, Complete Anatomy and Physiopedia give the T11–L2 spinous processes; Willard et al. (2012) describe its aponeurosis reaching the lower two thoracic and upper two or three lumbar spinous processes.
- Serratus posterior inferior and the latissimus dorsi aponeurosis: Willard et al. (2012) report that Bogduk and Macintosh found the serratus posterior inferior attached to the latissimus dorsi aponeurosis, whereas Vleeming et al. found attachments only to the deep lamina.
- Erector spinae lumbar/thoracic parts: Kenhub divides both iliocostalis lumborum and longissimus thoracis into lumbar and thoracic parts; StatPearls says each 'contains lumbar and thoracic sections'; Physiopedia 'Erector Spinae' labels lumbar and thoracic parts only for the longissimus thoracis; Complete Anatomy and TeachMeAnatomy do not divide them.

### 横突棘肌、节段小肌、腰方肌与腰大肌（m2）

来源之间的出入（discrepancies）：

- Multifidus direction convention: Kenhub, TeachMeAnatomy and Complete Anatomy call the caudal attachments (mammillary processes, sacrum, posterior superior iliac spine) the origin and the spinous processes the insertion, whereas Macintosh et al. and Bogduk et al., as summarised by Lonnemann et al. (PMC2716159), describe the fascicles as arising from the spinous processes and laminae and inserting caudally.
- Multifidus segments spanned: Kenhub says fascicles insert 2 to 5 levels above their origin and bridge three to six vertebral levels; Complete Anatomy says two to five segments; TeachMeAnatomy says 2-4 segments; Physiopedia gives insertion 2-4 levels above origin and says the deep layer spans 2 segments and other layers up to 4; the Macintosh description (via Lonnemann et al.) gives 2, 3, 4 and 5 levels for successive fascicles.
- Multifidus innervation: Kenhub, Complete Anatomy and Physiopedia name the medial branch of the dorsal (posterior) ramus, TeachMeAnatomy says only posterior rami, and Lonnemann et al. report Bogduk et al.'s unisegmental model alongside Wu et al.'s conclusion of polysegmental innervation.
- Multifidus unilateral action: Kenhub gives ipsilateral lateral flexion and contralateral rotation, Physiopedia gives contralateral rotation only, Complete Anatomy describes no unilateral action, Lonnemann et al. say all four layers may act unilaterally to rotate the lumbar spine laterally in the frontal plane, and Macintosh et al. (via Lonnemann et al.) suggested posterior sagittal rotation as its primary action.
- Multifidus depth: a scoping review (BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders) found research studies describing lumbar multifidus as superficial, deep or both, while atlases predominantly show it as deep.
- Multifidus attachments: Kenhub adds the posterior sacroiliac ligament; TeachMeAnatomy lists the common tendinous origin of the erector spinae; Lonnemann et al. add the iliac crest and the sacroiliac joint for fascicles from L3 and lower; Complete Anatomy and Physiopedia list neither the erector spinae aponeurosis nor the iliac crest.
- Lumbar rotatores: Physiopedia states rotatores lumborum are often inconsistent and can be replaced by deep multifidus fibres; Kenhub's table lists rotatores breves only from T2-T12 and TeachMeAnatomy says they are most prominent in the thoracic region, without describing the lumbar part. Kenhub gives the insertion as the laminae/spinous process one (breves) or two (longi) levels above, while Physiopedia gives the superior lamina for the brevis and the base of the spinous process two levels above for the longus.
- Intertransversarii innervation: Kenhub (dedicated page) gives posterior rami for the medial and anterior rami for the lateral lumbar intertransversarii, but Kenhub's deep back muscles overview gives anterior and posterior rami for lumbar intertransversarii; Complete Anatomy lists anterior and posterior rami for both medial and lateral groups; Radiopaedia's detailed text says both groups are supplied by anterior rami, while its own summary says anterior and posterior rami.
- Medial intertransversarii insertion: Complete Anatomy gives the mammillary processes of L2-L5; Radiopaedia says the mammillary process of the associated lumbar vertebrae (L1-L4); Kenhub says they attach between the accessory and mammillary processes of contiguous vertebrae L1-L4.
- Quadratus lumborum origin: Radiopaedia adds the transverse process of the L5 vertebra, whereas Kenhub, TeachMeAnatomy, Complete Anatomy, StatPearls and Physiopedia list only the iliac crest and the iliolumbar ligament (Complete Anatomy specifies the posterior half of the iliac crest; StatPearls and Physiopedia the inner lip).
- Quadratus lumborum rib insertion: Kenhub, TeachMeAnatomy and Complete Anatomy give the inferior border of the 12th rib; Physiopedia and Radiopaedia give the medial half of its inferior border; StatPearls gives the 12th rib's internal surface; Radiopaedia notes that the extent of the rib attachment varies.
- Quadratus lumborum innervation: Kenhub, TeachMeAnatomy, Radiopaedia and Complete Anatomy give T12 (subcostal) plus the anterior rami of L1-L4; StatPearls names the 12th thoracic intercostal, iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal nerves; Physiopedia lists the subcostal nerve, the iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal nerves (L1) and ventral rami of L2 and L3, without L4.
- Quadratus lumborum and the iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal nerves: StatPearls says the 12th thoracic, ilioinguinal and iliohypogastric nerves pass through and branch to the muscle, whereas Kenhub says the iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal nerves run on its ventral surface and Complete Anatomy places the muscle posterior to these nerves.
- Quadratus lumborum and respiration: TeachMeAnatomy, Radiopaedia, Complete Anatomy, Physiopedia and the Kenhub key-facts table say it fixes the 12th rib during inspiration; the Kenhub body text says it supports expiration; StatPearls says the mobility of the last rib prevents it transmitting force to the diaphragm and that it might influence forced expiration.
- Quadratus lumborum as an extensor and lateral flexor: Kenhub, TeachMeAnatomy, Radiopaedia and Complete Anatomy list lumbar extension, while StatPearls and Physiopedia report a force of only 10 N during extension (versus 100 N for the erector spinae and 150 N for the multifidus); for lateral tilt StatPearls gives 10 N, whereas Physiopedia says it contributes less than 10% of the force required for a coronal inclination; StatPearls also cites a cadaver study that raised doubts about its actions.
- Quadratus lumborum and low back pain: Kenhub calls overuse and strain one of the major causes of chronic lower back pain, whereas StatPearls states there is no consensus that altered quadratus lumborum tone is a primary cause of back pain.
- Psoas major origin: TeachMeAnatomy gives the vertebral bodies and transverse processes of T12-L5; Radiopaedia's summary gives T12-L5 but its gross anatomy section gives the T12-L4 discs and adjacent bodies and the L1-L5 transverse processes; Kenhub's table gives the bodies of T12-L4 and the transverse processes of L1-L5; StatPearls (psoas page) and Physiopedia describe a superficial part from the lower thoracic vertebrae and a deep part from the first four lumbar vertebrae; StatPearls (lumbar spine page) says it originates from the T12-L4 transverse processes.
- Psoas major innervation: Kenhub, TeachMeAnatomy and Radiopaedia give the anterior rami of L1-L3 (Radiopaedia: mainly L2), with Kenhub and Radiopaedia describing the supply as via the lumbar plexus; StatPearls and Physiopedia give L1-L4, Physiopedia describes the branches as arising before the rami form the lumbar plexus, and StatPearls adds small femoral nerve branches.
- Psoas major hip actions other than flexion: Kenhub lists lateral rotation of the thigh; Physiopedia lists hip adduction and external rotation; StatPearls is internally inconsistent, giving external rotation and abduction in its introduction but adduction in its Function section.
- Psoas major action on the lumbar spine: TeachMeAnatomy and Radiopaedia give lateral flexion; StatPearls says it flexes the spine, side-bends it ipsilaterally and rotates it to the opposite side; StatPearls says it increases lumbar lordosis, Kenhub says it maintains normal lordosis in standing, the ASLR study says it might contribute to regulating lordosis, and Bogduk (as cited in that study) held that its small moment arms give it little effect on the spine.
- Interspinales action: Complete Anatomy's quick facts say they extend the trunk, while Kenhub says they are only thought to assist extension and that their more important role is stabilisation.

### 神经（n1）

来源之间的出入（discrepancies）：

- Adult conus level: TeachMeAnatomy says the cord tapers off at the L2 vertebral level; StatPearls 'Neuroanatomy, Spinal Nerves' says 'typically at the L1 or L2 vertebral level'; StatPearls 'Anatomy, Back, Cauda Equina' says L1-L2; the StatPearls conus article gives an average at the middle third of L1 with a range from the middle third of T11 to the middle third of L3; Radiopaedia says most often at the L1/2 disc, with a level at or above the L2/3 disc considered normal and L3 equivocal; Physiopedia (Cauda Equina) says the cord ends around L1. Records therefore say 'usually L1–L2'.
- Filum terminale attachment: TeachMeAnatomy says it attaches to the vertebral bodies of the coccyx; the StatPearls conus article says it connects the conus to the coccyx via the coccygeal ligament; Radiopaedia says it extends down to the sacrum.
- Lumbar plexus roots: TeachMeAnatomy gives L1-L4 anterior rami plus a T12 contribution; Kenhub gives L1-L4 (T12 appears only in its image captions); StatPearls 'Neuroanatomy, Spinal Nerves' gives L1-L4; Radiopaedia says roots from T12 to L5.
- Lumbar plexus position: TeachMeAnatomy says it lies within the substance of psoas major and Elsevier Complete Anatomy says it forms inside psoas major; Kenhub describes it as close to psoas major.
- Sinuvertebral nerve origin: Radiopaedia says it is a branch of the primary ventral ramus and the grey ramus communicans; Elsevier Complete Anatomy says meningeal branches originate directly from the anterior rami (receiving grey rami input); Physiopedia says it originates from the ventral ramus; the European Spine Journal 2021 study describes a somatic root from the spinal nerve and a sympathetic root from the rami communicantes.
- Sinuvertebral nerve dural supply: Physiopedia specifies the anterior portion of the dura mater; Elsevier's lumbar meningeal-branch page says 'the ventral surface of the dural sac' (and also 'dura mater along the clivus', which looks like generic cranial wording); the European Spine Journal 2021 study says the dura mater; Radiopaedia does not mention the dura but adds the periosteum of the vertebral body.
- Superior cluneal nerve origin: Elsevier Complete Anatomy names the upper three lateral branches (L1-L3); StatPearls 'Cluneal Neuralgia' says contributions can range from T11 to L5; the ASRA article says T12-L5 (citing Neilson et al., with L1-L3 contributing most often); Isu et al. (Neurospine 2018, citing Konno et al.) say T12 to L5.
- Superior cluneal nerve distance from the midline at the iliac crest: StatPearls gives about 6.6 cm for the medial branch at the osteofibrous tunnel; the ASRA article gives approximately 7 cm; Isu et al. (Neurospine 2018) give 3-4 cm for the medial branch and 7-8 cm for the middle branch where they penetrate the thoracolumbar fascia. The record says 'usually about 7 cm'.
- Facet joint dual innervation wording: StatPearls 'Lumbosacral Facet Syndrome' (L3-L4 joint from the L2 and L3 medial branches), Physiopedia 'Lumbar Facet Syndrome' (one at the same level, one a level above) and Radiopaedia (each medial branch supplies its own level and the level below, e.g. L1 to L1/2 and L2/3) agree; Physiopedia 'Facet Joints' says the facet receives a medial branch 'from the spinal nerve above the facet and the nerve below', which could be read as a different numbering.
- Medial branch position: Physiopedia 'Lumbar Facet Syndrome' says the L1–L4 medial branches run across the top of the transverse process one level below the named spinal nerve, and Elsevier's general medial-branch page places each branch at the root of the subjacent transverse process. Elsevier's L4 medial-branch page is internally inconsistent: its Quick Facts place the branch over the subjacent fifth lumbar transverse and superior articular processes, while its Course section names the fourth lumbar transverse process. Dupont et al. say the L1–L4 medial branches cross the superior aspect of 'their' transverse processes.
- Dorsal root ganglion position: Elsevier's 'Lumbar Nerves' page says the ganglion is at the distal end of each posterior root, while Elsevier's 'Lumbar Spinal Roots & Ganglia' page says the proximal end; StatPearls calls it an oval enlargement 'just outside the cord'. Silverstein et al. (IJSS 2015) found the ganglion centre in the medial zone of the foramen at L1-L4 and the lateral zone at L5, and cite Kikuchi et al. for L5 ganglia being 19.2% intraspinal, 72.8% intraforaminal and 8% extraforaminal.
- L4 key muscle: the ISNCSCI worksheet and Physiopedia's ASIA page use ankle dorsiflexors (tibialis anterior); StatPearls 'Sciatica' links ankle dorsiflexion weakness to L4; StatPearls 'Lumbosacral Radiculopathy' gives the quadriceps (knee extension) for L4 and tibialis anterior with extensor hallucis longus (dorsiflexion) for L5; Physiopedia 'Myotomes' lists both knee extension and ankle dorsiflexion for L4.
- Knee extension root: the ISNCSCI worksheet assigns knee extensors to L3; StatPearls 'Lumbosacral Radiculopathy' assigns the quadriceps to L4; Physiopedia 'Myotomes' lists knee extension under both L3 and L4.
- L5 movements: the ISNCSCI worksheet uses long toe extensors; Physiopedia 'Myotomes' lists great toe extension, ankle dorsiflexion and hip abduction; StatPearls 'Sciatica' gives hip abduction and big toe extension; StatPearls 'Lumbosacral Radiculopathy' gives tibialis anterior and extensor hallucis longus (dorsiflexion).
- Patellar reflex level: Lees and Hurwitz give L3/L4; the Toronto Metropolitan University nursing text says primarily L4 but can include L2 and L3; StatPearls 'Lumbosacral Radiculopathy' links the knee jerk to L4.
- Achilles reflex level: StatPearls 'Lumbosacral Radiculopathy' and 'Sciatica' link it to S1; Lees and Hurwitz give S1/S2; the Toronto Metropolitan University nursing text says primarily S1 but can include S2 and L5.
- S1 sensory area: Physiopedia 'Dermatomes' gives the sole, plantar toes, posterior leg, lateral heel and lateral foot; StatPearls 'Lumbosacral Radiculopathy' gives the lateral malleolus and lateral foot; the ISNCSCI key sensory point (Physiopedia ASIA page) is the lateral heel (calcaneus).
- L4 sensory area: Physiopedia 'Dermatomes' includes the dorsum of the foot in L4 as well as the medial leg and medial big toe, while StatPearls 'Lumbosacral Radiculopathy' gives the medial malleolus and medial foot for L4 and the dorsum of the foot for L5.
- Sciatic nerve relation to piriformis: TeachMeAnatomy and Elsevier say it emerges below (beneath) piriformis; StatPearls 'Sciatica' says it courses inferior and anterior to piriformis.
- Sinuvertebral nerve presence: Elsevier's meningeal-branch page describes meningeal branches at all spinal levels except C1–C5, whereas the European Spine Journal 2021 cadaveric study found no sinuvertebral nerve in 9 of 48 lumbar canals and two at the same level in 4 of 48.
- Sinuvertebral nerve entry: Elsevier and Physiopedia describe it re-entering the vertebral canal through the intervertebral foramen; the European Spine Journal 2021 study describes it entering near the inferior vertebral notch.
- Lumbar lateral branch muscles: Wheeless' Textbook of Orthopaedics says the lateral branches go primarily to iliocostalis lumborum and assigns longissimus to the intermediate branch; Elsevier's lateral-branch page says the lateral branches run deep to and innervate the iliocostalis and longissimus components of the lumbar erector spinae.
- Femoral nerve division: TeachMeAnatomy and StatPearls say it divides into anterior and posterior divisions about 4 cm below the inguinal ligament; Elsevier says it is separated into superficial and deep divisions by the lateral circumflex femoral artery and gives no distance.
- Superior cluneal nerve layer of fascia: Elsevier says the lateral branches pierce the dorsal layer of the thoracolumbar fascia; the ASRA article says they traverse the superficial layer; Radiopaedia says they travel underneath the fascia.

### 运动与生物力学（mv）

来源之间的出入（discrepancies）：

- Lumbar range of motion: Physiopedia ('Functional Anatomy of the Lumbar Spine and Abdominal Wall') gives flexion 60°, extension 25°, lateral flexion 20–30° and rotation 1–5°, while Kenhub ('Intervertebral joint') gives flexion 55°, extension 30°, lateral flexion 20–30° and axial rotation 1–2°; neither says whether the rotation figure is per side or total.
- Facet angle: Physiopedia ('Facet Joints') describes lumbar facets as at right angles to the transverse plane and 45° to the frontal plane, while Kenhub ('Intervertebral joint') attributes the greater lumbar flexion range to 'the 45° orientation of the articular process' in the sagittal plane; both agree the lumbar articular surfaces are essentially sagittal.
- Segmental share: Kenhub ('Lumbosacral joint') says that before puberty the lumbosacral joint accounts for about 75% of total lumbar flexion–extension (about 18°), whereas the in vitro study by Cook, Yeager & Cheng 2015 found L4–L5 and L5–S1 both significantly greater than the other levels, without singling out L5–S1; the Kenhub figure is also internally hard to reconcile with a total lumbar flexion–extension range of about 85° on both Physiopedia (60° + 25°) and Kenhub (55° + 30°).
- Coupling direction: Physiopedia ('Facet Joints') first states that lumbar lateral bending couples with opposite-side rotation, then that lateral bending as the first movement couples with same-direction rotation (and rotation as the first movement with opposite-direction lateral bending), so the page contradicts itself; Physiopedia 'Psoas Major' gives ipsilateral side-bending with contralateral rotation as its example; Cook 2003 reports that coupling models are discrepant between manual therapy approaches; and Liebsch & Wilke 2025 found only a tendency toward reproducibility at L2–L5 and dominant ipsilateral lateral bending during primary axial rotation at L4–S1.
- Quadratus lumborum and extension: Physiopedia 'Functional Anatomy of the Lumbar Spine and Abdominal Wall' lists bilateral trunk extension as an action, but Physiopedia 'Quadratus Lumborum' states that its extension force (about 10 N) seems too small to extend the lumbar spine in the sagittal plane.
- Quadratus lumborum and lateral flexion: Physiopedia 'Lumbar Anatomy' states that 'pure lateral flexion is brought about only by the quadratus lumborum', whereas Physiopedia 'Quadratus Lumborum' states that during a lateral tilt of the trunk it supplies less than 10% of the force required for the coronal inclination, and Physiopedia 'Functional Anatomy of the Lumbar Spine and Abdominal Wall' and 'Psoas Major' also list psoas major and the external oblique as lateral flexors.
